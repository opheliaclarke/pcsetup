3 The pedestal — drawings for your carpenter

A simple open rectangular frame in hardwood, on four locking castors. The case sits on it. The middle must be completely open — the graphics card breathes through the floor of the case, and a solid base would suffocate it.

The one rule that cannot be broken: no board, no panel, no cross-piece anywhere in the middle. Four rails around the edge and nothing else. If your carpenter puts a solid top on it, the ₹7-lakh graphics card cannot breathe.
About the mesh — you had the right idea, with one adjustment. Stretch mesh across the open middle so nothing can fall through and no foot or paw can go in. But make it coarse — expanded metal or a grille with roughly 5–10 mm holes, the sort of thing you would see on a speaker grille. Do not use a fine dust mesh. The case already has its own fine dust filter directly above, and stacking a second fine mesh under it just doubles the resistance on the one air path your most expensive component has. The pedestal mesh is there to stop objects, not dust — the case's own filter does the dust.

Wood is fine — use hardwood. Teak, sheesham or sal, in 1.5 × 1.5 inch section. At 40 kg spread over four corners this is nowhere near the limit of hardwood; steel buys you nothing here except a bigger bill. Two things to get right in wood: join the corners properly (half-lap or mortise, or plain butt joints with steel corner brackets — not just screwed end-on), and make sure the castor plates land on solid timber with room for all four screws.

What not to use: MDF or particle board — the screws holding the castors pull out of it and it sags under load. Plywood is acceptable for the rails if it's 18 mm or thicker, but hardwood is easier for a carpenter to join well.
Height correction. An earlier version of this page said 1.5 inches total including castors. That was wrong — you cannot have a 1.5 inch thick rail and a castor and still be 1.5 inches tall. The real number is 3 to 4 inches: about 1.5 in of hardwood plus a 1.5–2.5 in castor. That is fine now, because you are programming the desk's minimum height yourself. Measure the finished pedestal with the case on it before you set that limit.

Castors — 2 fixed at the back, 2 swivel at the front

Not all four swivel, and not all four fixed. This is how a trolley is built and it's the right answer here:

  • Two FIXED castors at the back (roll forward and back only). They make the machine track dead straight when you pull it out from under the desk. All-swivel wanders sideways as you pull, which is annoying with a 40 kg tower and cables attached.
  • Two SWIVEL castors at the front, both with brakes. These let you steer and angle it out of the corner. Lock them once it's parked.
  • Ask for 60–75 mm wheel diameter, rubber or PU tread — not hard nylon. Small hard wheels catch on floor joints and mark the floor. Rubber also kills the fan and pump vibration that travels into the floor.

If your floor is uneven or you have to cross a threshold, go all-four-swivel-with-brakes instead — you lose straight tracking but gain manoeuvrability. Either is fine. What is not fine is unbraked castors: a 40 kg machine that rolls when nudged will eventually pull on a cable.

The mesh — and your pull-out idea

Use ¼ inch (6 mm) welded galvanised wire mesh, or expanded aluminium mesh with similar openings. Any hardware shop in India has it by the foot, cheap. It's about 80% open area, so it costs you almost no airflow, and it's stiff enough to span the opening without sagging. Staple or screw it into a shallow rebate on the underside of the frame so the top face stays flat.

Your pull-out idea is good — but you're aiming it at the wrong filter.

Build the pull-out drawer if you like: a shallow groove down the inside of both long rails, and a light mesh sub-frame that slides in from the front like an AC filter. It's easy for a carpenter and it can't hurt.

But know what it's for. This mesh is an object guard, not a dust filter. At 6 mm openings it will not clog and will not need weekly cleaning — dust goes straight through it. The filter that actually needs cleaning weekly is the case's own dust filter, which sits directly above it. That one is fine, it does clog, and it is the only thing standing between floor dust and your ₹7-lakh card.

So the real design goal is: make sure the case's filter can slide out with the machine still sitting on the pedestal. Keep the front rail flush and clear, and you clean it in 30 seconds a week without lifting 40 kg. If you also make the pedestal mesh removable, that's a bonus for the once-a-year deep clean.
Do not use AC filter material or fly-screen mesh here. Those are fine filters. Stacking a fine filter directly under the case's own fine filter doubles the resistance on the one air path the flow-through card has. Coarse mesh above, fine filter in the case — never two fine layers.
Tell the carpenter, in one line: "Make a rectangular open frame, 22 inches by 13 inches outside, from 1.5 by 1.5 inch hardwood. Completely open in the middle — no board, no cross-piece. Coarse mesh stretched across the opening. Four locking castors underneath, one at each corner. Total height with the castors 3 to 4 inches. Two fixed castors at the back, two braked swivel castors at the front. It has to hold 60 kg. Flat top, nothing standing proud."

5 Step by step — moving from the Supernova

Before you touch anything. Shut the machine down, switch the power supply off at its own switch, pull the wall plug, then press and hold the case power button for 10 seconds to drain what's left. Work on a table, not carpet. Touch a bare metal part of the case now and then to discharge yourself. Never work with the machine plugged in.

  1. Photograph everything first

    Take 15–20 photos of the inside of the Supernova from every angle before you unplug a single cable — especially where the small front-panel wires connect to the motherboard. You will need these later.

  2. Unplug the graphics cards and take them out

    Unplug their power cables first. Press the little latch at the end of each PCIe slot, then lift each card straight out. Put them on a soft surface, fan side up.

    Look at the PRO 6000's power cable now while it's in your hand. Is the plug that goes into the card straight or bent at 90°? Is the other end stamped Type 4? That answer decides whether you need to buy a new cable at all.

  3. Take out the power supply and the drives

    Unplug everything from the power supply, then unscrew it from the back of the Supernova. Unplug the SSDs and drives, keep their screws.

  4. Take out the motherboard — with the CPU and cooler still on it

    Unplug the front-panel wires, USB and audio headers. Unscrew the CPU radiator from the Supernova but leave the cooler attached to the CPU. Then unscrew the motherboard's 9 screws and lift the whole assembly out — board, CPU, cooler and radiator together.

    Do not remove the CPU from its socket. There is no reason to, and bent pins are the easiest expensive mistake in this whole job.

  5. Unbox the Lian Li and strip it down

    Take off both glass panels (lift them out, don't force them), the top panel and the front panel. Put the glass somewhere safe, flat, where nothing can fall on it. Take the accessory box out of the case.

  6. Set the motherboard tray to the MIDDLE position

    The tray can be mounted at three heights. Undo its screws, slide it to the middle setting, and screw it back down.

    This is the single most important step in the whole build. Middle gives you room for the radiator above and air for the graphics card below. The top setting won't fit your radiator; the bottom setting starves your ₹7-lakh card.

  7. Fit the three floor fans

    Screw the three ARCTIC P14 fans onto the floor tray so they blow upward, into the case. The label side of a fan faces the direction the air comes out — so labels point up.

    Push all three as far towards the back of the case as the mounting slots allow, and as far inward (towards the motherboard) as they go. Centred or forward and one of the three blows past the nose of the graphics card into empty space.

  8. Fit the power supply in the cable room

    Mount the AX1600i in the second room with its fan facing the mesh side panel so it breathes outside air. Plug the cables you'll need into the power supply now, while there's still room to work: the big 24-pin, two CPU/EPS cables, and separate cables for each graphics card.

    Use a separate cable for each graphics card power connector. Never run one cable with two plugs on it to a 600 W card.

  9. Install the motherboard

    Check the brass standoffs are in the right holes for an ATX board, fit the rear I/O shield if your board has a loose one, then lower the board in and do up its 9 screws. Snug, not tight.

  10. Route the two CPU power cables NOW — before the radiator goes in

    Do not skip the order here. The two 8-pin CPU power connectors sit along the top edge of the motherboard. Once the roof radiator is in, you cannot get your hand in there. Plug them in and dress them before the radiator.

  11. Fit the RTX 5090's radiator in the roof

    Fans on the radiator, then the whole assembly screwed to the roof bracket, set to blow out of the case. Route its two hoses down towards where the graphics card will sit. Don't kink them.

  12. Fit the CPU radiator on the side

    It mounts vertically at the front of the main room, set to blow into the case. Fit the two small blanking plates from the accessory box to close off the unused gap — otherwise the fans just recycle air instead of pulling it through the mesh.

  13. Fit the rear fan

    One 120 mm fan from the Supernova, at the back, blowing out.

  14. Install the RTX PRO 6000 FIRST — in the LOWER slot

    Counting the slot openings from the top, it goes in the 6th one. Line it up, press down evenly until the latch clicks, then screw its bracket down.

    Always this card first, before the 5090. The lower slot's release latch is reached from above, and once the 5090 is in you can't get at it. If you ever need to remove the PRO 6000 later, the 5090 has to come out first.

  15. Install the RTX 5090 in the TOP slot

    Top slot, then screw its bracket down and attach its radiator hoses neatly. Fit the anti-sag bracket that came in the case box to this card.

  16. Fit the support stick under the PRO 6000

    Adjust it so it just touches the underside of the card — enough to stop it drooping, not enough to lift it. Position it between two floor fans, never on top of one.

  17. Connect all the power

    24-pin to the motherboard. Both 8-pin CPU cables (already done in step 10). One power cable to each graphics card, pushed in until it clicks.

    Push each graphics card plug fully home and check it visually. A half-seated 600 W connector is how these cards catch fire. If it doesn't click, take it out and try again — never force it.

  18. Connect the small stuff

    Front panel power switch and LEDs, front USB, fan cables to the motherboard headers. Use your photos from step 1 for the front-panel wires.

  19. Tidy the cables

    Everything in the cable room, velcro strapped every 4 inches. Keep the two graphics card power cables separated from each other, not bundled together. Nothing loose in the main room where fans can catch it.

  20. Fit the side dust filter, then close up

    Stick the magnetic filter over the side intake area. Put the panels back on — glass last, and don't over-tighten the panel screws, which is a known cause of the glass cracking later.

  21. Put it on the pedestal and check the desk

    Lift it on, lock the castors. Now drive the desk to its lowest setting with the machine in place removed — measure the gap first with the bay empty — then set your new desk minimum height above it.

  22. First boot — into BIOS only

    Plug in and power on. Go straight into BIOS. Turn on Above 4G Decoding and Resizable BAR. Check both graphics cards are detected and the CPU temperature looks normal at idle. Then shut down and check nothing is rubbing or loose.

  23. Boot into the OS and check the cards

    Run nvidia-smi. Both cards should appear, and the PRO 6000 should report 96 GB.

  24. Set the fan control up BEFORE any real work

    This matters more than everything else on this page. Left alone, these cards sit at about 30% fan speed while reaching 85–92 °C, and then drop off the bus. Owners who took manual control measured 15–19 °C lower. Install LACT (or a small script) and set a curve: 40 °C → 30%, 50 °C → 55%, 55 °C → 75%, 60 °C → 90%, 65 °C and above → 100%. Set both cards to about 80% power limit on day one.

  25. Run it hard for two hours and watch

    Load both cards properly. Watch GPU temperature, GPU memory temperature and the motherboard temperature. If a card goes over 85 °C while its fan is under 70%, stop and fix the fan curve before doing anything else.

  26. Put a monthly reminder in your phone: clean the floor filter

    Slide it out from the front — that's why the pedestal has a clear front edge — rinse, dry completely, slide back. That filter is the only air supply your most expensive component has.
